Thousands of new mums suffering undiagnosed PTSD after childbirth

Tens of thousands of new mothers across the UK may be living with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) after childbirth without ever being diagnosed, according to a new report from the University of East Anglia.

The researchers warn that symptoms of trauma after pregnancy or birth can be missed, misunderstood, or wrongly labelled as something else during routine postnatal GP appointments, meaning some women may not get the right support at the right time.

The report lands in the wake of two major reviews into maternity and neonatal services in England led by Baroness Amos and Donna Ockenden, adding to growing concern about how the NHS identifies and responds to maternal mental health needs after birth.

What is childbirth-related PTSD?

PTSD is often associated with experiencing or witnessing a frightening event, and it can also develop after a difficult pregnancy, labour, birth, or postnatal complications.

Symptoms can include:
• Flashbacks or intrusive memories
• Nightmares
• Feeling constantly on edge, anxious or panicky
• Avoiding reminders of the birth, including medical appointments
• Persistent negative thoughts or feelings of guilt and shame

Importantly, the UEA team says PTSD can happen even after a medically uncomplicated birth, because trauma is not only about what happened clinically, but also how safe, heard and supported someone felt during the experience.

How common is PTSD after birth?

Lead researcher and GP Dr Megan Foreman, from UEA’s Norwich Medical School, said research suggests around one in 20 postnatal women develop PTSD. Across the UK, that could translate to tens of thousands of mothers.

The researchers argue this may only be the visible part of a much bigger issue, describing severe outcomes as the “tip of the iceberg”, with many more women living with significant distress but struggling to access help.

The report also points to recent UK figures showing suicide remained the leading cause of death among women between six weeks and one year after pregnancy during 2021 to 2023.

Why might PTSD be missed after childbirth?

The report suggests that GPs are often expected to act as the gateway into specialist mental health support for new mums, but may not have enough information, time, or the right tools to make accurate assessments.

Dr Foreman said: “The biggest issue is that GPs are effectively ‘diagnosing in the dark’ because they are not equipped with the information or tools to make proper assessments.”

Although birth trauma is mentioned in national guidance from NICE and NHS England, the analysis argues that practical support is lacking. For example, there is limited guidance for GPs on how to measure severity, assess risk, and decide who needs specialist treatment.

The report also suggests that some healthcare professionals, including GPs, may not feel confident or adequately trained to support women affected by birth trauma.

Add in pressures such as appointment time limits and staff shortages, and it becomes easier for symptoms to go unpicked.

PTSD or postnatal depression? Why getting the label right matters

The researchers also warn that some women may be misdiagnosed with postnatal depression.

That matters because the most effective treatment for PTSD is usually specialist psychological therapy, not medication alone.

The analysis notes that antidepressants are generally less effective for PTSD than specialist psychological interventions, and they are not recommended by NICE as a first-line treatment for PTSD.

The hidden impact on families and future pregnancies

Untreated childbirth-related PTSD can affect how a mum feels day to day, but it can also have longer-term consequences for the whole family.

Dr Foreman explained that some women may:

• Seek repeated reassurance from healthcare professionals for themselves or their baby
• Avoid healthcare services altogether, including in future pregnancies
• Request an elective Caesarean section next time to regain a sense of control
• Reject recommended care, or choose a home birth even in high-risk pregnancies, to avoid interventions linked to a previous traumatic experience

What next?

The team is calling for urgent work to develop clearer GP guidance, including a validated assessment tool that could be used during the routine six to eight week postnatal appointment to help identify symptoms of childbirth-related PTSD.

However, they stress that this must be handled with care. Dr Foreman said that many women attend appointments with their newborns, often combining their own check with baby examinations or vaccinations, and some may not feel ready to talk about a traumatic birth so soon. The report warns that asking the wrong questions at the wrong time could leave women feeling unsupported, or even retraumatised.

Rather than simply adding more questions to an already pressured appointment, the authors argue that any future guidance needs to be backed up with better GP training on birth trauma and PTSD, improved awareness of symptoms and risk factors, and clear referral pathways into specialist perinatal mental health services and trauma-informed therapy.

If this story feels familiar, you are not alone

If you are struggling after birth, especially with intrusive memories, panic, sleep disruption due to nightmares, or avoidance of anything linked to your labour or hospital stay, it is worth speaking to a health professional.

You can start with your GP, midwife, or health visitor and ask specifically about birth trauma and PTSD symptoms. If you do not feel heard, you are allowed to ask again, ask for a longer appointment, or request a referral to specialist perinatal mental health support.
