Stone Soup (Nail Broth)

Author: Swedish Folk Tales  |  Region: Swedish  |  Reading Level: Easy

Stone Soup, known in this Swedish telling as Nail Broth, is a short tale about a hungry soldier who talks a reluctant old woman out of a meal without ever directly asking for one. Claiming he can make soup from nothing but a nail and water, he coaxes her, step by step, into contributing salt, flour, an egg, and lard, until he has cooked himself a proper meal.

They tell of a soldier who came to an old woman’s house and asked her to give him something to eat. She told him that she doesn’t have any food in the house, so the soldier said: “if so, give me at least a pan and some water so that I may make nail broth.” Granny gives him that, and he takes the pan and puts an iron nail in it, then pours water and puts it on the fire. When the water got hot, then he asks from the granny a bit of salt, and she gives it to him, and so he salts it. When the water boiled, then he asks from the granny a bit of flour; grandma gives him that too, just so that she could see the wonder: how can one make nail broth, and so he poured it into the water and stirred; then he asked for an egg, and breaks it into the starch; then he asked for a bit of lard, and greased it a bit; and then he took it from the fire and took the nail out, and eats the nail broth.

The Origins of Stone Soup (Nail Broth)

This is a Swedish version of a folk tale type found across Europe under names like stone soup or button soup, in which a clever traveler tricks a stingy or wary host into providing a full meal one small ingredient at a time. In the Swedish telling collected here, the trick centers on a nail rather than a stone, giving the tale its alternate title, Nail Broth. Versions of this story appear in many European folk traditions, and its enduring popularity comes from its simple structure and its sly commentary on hospitality and persuasion.

Moral and Themes of Stone Soup (Nail Broth)

Nail Broth is a story about resourcefulness and the power of suggestion rather than direct demand. The soldier never once asks the old woman outright for food; instead, he lets her curiosity about his impossible-sounding nail broth draw out one ingredient after another until he has cooked a real meal from her own pantry. The tale rewards cleverness and patience over a blunt request, and it gently pokes fun at how easily people can be led along once their curiosity is engaged.

Frequently Asked Questions About Stone Soup (Nail Broth)

What is the moral of Stone Soup (Nail Broth)?

The story shows how cleverness and patience can accomplish what a direct request might not. The soldier never asks the old woman for food outright; he lets her curiosity about his strange nail broth draw out each ingredient in turn.

Why is it called ‘nail broth’ in this version?

The soldier claims he can make soup using only an iron nail and water, which is why this Swedish telling is called Nail Broth rather than the more familiar stone soup. He puts the nail in the pan as his starting point, then gradually asks for more.

What ingredients does the old woman end up giving the soldier?

Believing she is only helping him with his strange nail broth, she gives him salt, flour, an egg, and a bit of lard, one at a time, as he asks for each. By the end these ingredients combine into an actual, edible soup.

Does the old woman realize she has been tricked?

The story does not say directly, but it notes she gives him the flour partly out of curiosity, wanting to see how one could possibly make broth from a nail. She watches the process unfold rather than being told outright that he is cooking a real meal.

What happens to the nail at the end of the story?

After the soup is finished, the soldier takes the pan off the fire, removes the nail, and eats the broth he has made, implying the nail itself contributed nothing and was only ever a pretext to get the old woman’s ingredients.
